In primary school, I couldn't wait to be done with primary school. In secondary school, I couldn't wait to be done with secondary school. In university, I couldn't wait to be done with university. You see the trend now, don't you? While serving, I couldn't wait to be done; after my first paycheck, I couldn't wait for the next paycheck. I think we are creatures with a default waiting setting. You waited to be born, you waited for your teeth to grow, and you waited to take your first steps, and here we are still waiting, waiting for our dreams to be actualized, waiting so badly for that version of yourself to become reality. You are not alone, I promise you. Forget the fact I'm a Liverpool fan; trust me, you are not walking alone, but you already know that, and you might say, "Well, the previous examples had a set date; I mean, primary school couldn't pass 6 years, secondary school also couldn't be longer than 6 years, and university at all, at all, 5-6 years if you were studying a 4-year course, 6-7 if you were studying a 5-year course."

It was all so structured, a class after the other, until you came to the end, but now you are in a different phase; there's no set date for the actualization of your dreams, you don't know when that scholarship will come or that perfect job, you don't even know what steps to take to get there. It's not class after class; there is no tutor except life, and life doesn't tutor well. That teacher you are always happy when they don't come to school, but life is always present, and you don't know when the class is over. The only clock that exists is your time, your biological clock, and 2 years in the same classroom leaves you wondering if you learned anything or you are that dull student at the back of the classroom, wondering how everyone is all smiles and full of questions when you don't understand the gibberish the teacher is saying. Mannnn, breathe. I know that's you. I know that's all of us actually.

I would argue that the problem is not the wait but your attitude during the wait. Bold statement. I know I'm full of that these days, but think about it. Let me paint a scenario: You are a parent with two wonderful children. You promised them both something special. A phone, a tab, a computer, or ice cream, and each day you come back home, one of them is always so excited to greet you, runs to you and jumps into your arms, welcomes you with a 21-gun salute or the equivalent in civilian terms, helps to ease your stress, and prepares your signature dish, without even knowing whether you got the gift or not, and on the other hand, the second's attitude is determined by whether you got the gift or not, moody and grumpy if you are empty-handed, and they give you that stare that says, "Why did you even come home, sef?" Happy if you are not empty-handed, even though the happiness is determined by what importance they place on the gift you got. Who would you genuinely feel encouraged gifting? Well, you might be biased, but who would you want to give the best of the best? If you are still biased now, it's fine. Na so your papa do you? It's fine, but I hope you do understand the scenario: that one gets favored over the other because of their behavior in the waiting phase. Don't only see it from the parent/guardian's perspective. What child do you think will receive their gift and will be able to boldly say "IT WAS WORTH THE WAIT"?

I have thought about this title for a long time, trust me, so I've also been in the waiting phase in writing this letter to you, and my question to myself in all my waiting phase was, "How many can I boldly say 'IT WAS WORTH THE WAIT'?" Why wait if it isn't worth it? You may say, "I don't want to wait, but I don't have a choice." Well, now that you realize you don't have a choice but to wait, how did that affect your behavior during the wait? The men of the Night's Watch say, "Night gathers, and now my watch begins," and at death they say, "And now his watch is over." Imagine if he absconded, because the wait was too long and morning never came, or because the night was too dark and he couldn't find his way? He saved himself, but he missed out on a great honor? Do we also do that to ourselves in our waiting period? Do we quit because it seems the wait never ends, or the wait seems to be going on for too long? The Bible talks about a prophet called Habakkuk. Let me tell you about him shortly: he questioned God about injustice and suffering, wondering in uncertainty, but yet he says something quite profound, Habakkuk 2:1, "I will stand upon my watch, and set me upon the tower, and will watch to see what he will say unto me." He does not have all the answers when he takes his position. He stands where he is precisely because he is waiting for an answer, and God responds with "For the vision is yet for an appointed time, but at the end it shall speak, and not lie: though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come, it will not tarry" (Habakkuk 2:3). And then I think to myself, perhaps the watchman/waiter is not someone who has never doubted; perhaps it is someone who has doubted, questioned, protested, and yet chosen to remain at his post, to at the end say, "IT WAS WORTH THE WAIT & NOW, MY WATCH IS OVER." Let me also talk to you about Horatius Cocles. Publius Horatius Cocles was an officer in the army of the early Roman Republic who defended the Pons Sublicius from the invading army of the Etruscan king Lars Porsena of Clusium in the late sixth century BC, during the war between Rome and Clusium. You may not understand what all that means (do read about him, if you can), but just know, he defended the Tiber/Sublician bridge against an invading army even while others dismantled the bridge. It is said he asked a memorable question: "And how can man die better than facing fearful odds?" This is a man who doesn't know whether someone is coming to save him or if he would survive that encounter or not, but yet in that moment of waiting he made it WORTH THE WAIT for others to survive.

Maybe I'm saying rubbish; truthfully, I don't know, but I've always wanted to say this to you, my friend, like a pregnant woman: aren't we all pregnant with ideas of what we will do when everything clicks, when we get that money we are looking for, when we get that yes, that person? But don't forget it takes a pregnant woman 9 months to deliver, and despite that she must go through prenatal development stages (germinal, embryonic, and fetal), trimesters, and perinatal/birth stages. She must go through each one with the demands it requests of her, all for the birth of an offspring; there is no shortcut to deliver before these 40 weeks, except if you want a stillborn or premature birth, which is usually not in consideration unless the specialists decide so. The pregnant woman doesn't stop being pregnant because she's tired of waiting; she doesn't wake up one day and decide, "I'm no more going to deliver this offspring of mine, because I'm tired of waiting." She waits patiently, devotedly, all for the promise of an offspring. Consider we are also like the pregnant woman without a due date. That idea that you are cooking might take 9 months, 10 months, or 12 months. Nobody can tell you that the life you've been trying to build will arrive in nine months. There is no ultrasound to show you that your sacrifices are developing properly, no medical examination to confirm that your ambitions are healthy, no doctor to tell you that you have just three more weeks of suffering before your circumstances change. Yes, you have given yourself a deadline, but you are not in control of your destiny; you don't know what is written, and you can only act accordingly. And so, in your days of waiting, do not be found wanting, that you didn't give your best to nursing that idea of an offspring, for you know not when opportunity might strike, and trust me.

You may want to ask now. Okay, so how do I wait? What is the right attitude?

I do understand. We are still not where we want to be, and sometimes, the little progress we have made becomes almost insulting when compared to the enormity of what we hoped to achieve. You have worked for three years, and all you have to show for it is a slightly better salary. You have spent years trying to build something, and it seems everyone else has somehow managed to build theirs faster. You look at your friends, their marriages, promotions, accomplishments, and the lives they have created, and you begin to wonder what exactly you've been doing with yours.

And so, what was once a dream becomes a burden. What once brought you excitement now brings you anxiety, and the person who once couldn't wait to begin the journey suddenly cannot wait for it to end. I hope you don't mistake the loss of something you desperately wanted for evidence that you are incapable of wanting, loving, or building anything ever again. Perhaps the hardest part of waiting is accepting that we cannot always determine what happens at the end of it. But does the possibility of losing something mean that we should never attempt to bring it into existence? If we knew that every relationship could end, would we refuse to love? If we knew that every business could fail, would we refuse to build? If we knew that every ambition could be frustrated, would we refuse to dream? What would become of us if we refused to begin anything whose ending we could not guarantee?

I once more quote something I heard, from Re:Zero:

> "Fight, even if you have regrets, even if you have remorse… Cling greedily to the victory you have within your sight."\
> (*Re:Zero − Starting Life in Another World*)

I am telling you to be strong while waiting no matter what it might cost, because it will cost you a lot. Just because something seems impossible doesn't mean it is. When you can't see a way forward, it's easy to assume there isn't one. Especially after disappointment, rejection, or years of feeling stuck. The danger is letting that decide what's possible for you. Your experience tells you what has happened so far. It can't tell you every person you'll meet, every opportunity you'll find, or what you become capable of doing; something you couldn't handle years ago may be within your reach today. A life you once couldn't imagine may become possible through decisions you haven't made yet. I want you to remember something: all through your life, someone has waited for you. Your mother, whom you were brought out from, waited for you to be born. Your parents/guardian waited for you to be done with school, to move up to the next grade; they waited for you to be able to speak, to be able to walk, and to be able to stand on your own. While we have waited all our lives, there are others who have spent their whole lives waiting for us. Wouldn't giving up now be a disrespect to them? Interestingly enough, I saw a quote on Twitter: "If you realize how many people are rooting for you in life, it would change your mindset. The interviewer wants you to ace it, because they don't want to interview someone else; the person you want to go on a date with wants to have an amazing date with you." So, what is the conclusion of the whole matter? Do not abandon yourself in the process of waiting; you owe that to yourself, the person you are waiting for, and the person waiting for you.

Wait, don't go… But if you must, remember. You were once a life the world had not yet seen. And you were becoming, long before anyone could hold you in their arms. Perhaps you still are. So strive as much as possible to say, or for those waiting to say, "You were worth the wait", "It was worth the wait".

This is getting quite long, and I fear I have to round it up, before you come at me for exhausting your short attention span, but I have to ask you, reading this long essay after so many days, was it worth the wait? Will you wait again for something similar?

Have a great week, my friend.

Yours, in waiting
